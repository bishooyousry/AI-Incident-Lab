# Facilitator guide — AI INCIDENT LAB: THE POINT OF NO RETURN (online)

**Goal:** participants practise reading evidence from real, documented AI failures, finding the root causes, choosing safeguards that work *outside* the AI’s own claims, and making and defending a stop / restrict / continue decision.
**Format:** individual or team, about 4–5 minutes per case file (three cases ≈ 13–15 minutes) + 5–7 minutes of debrief. For a shorter session, ask everyone to open any two cases.

## Know the source before you present
- The three cases and every fact in the game come from the supplied kit, and each case shows its source link: **Mata v. Avianca** (2023, U.S. District Court order) · **Moffatt v. Air Canada** (2024, tribunal decision summary) · **NEDA Tessa** (2023, WIRED report). Open the links yourself before presenting — the game does not verify them.
- Exhibit wording and the choices are educational reconstructions, not direct quotations. This is educational practice, not professional legal or health advice. Tessa was not a ChatGPT-style generative chatbot — say so.
- The kit contains no facilitator notes or debrief questions: the questions below are suggestions.

## What each case is (from the kit)
1. **Legal services — “The Cases That Never Existed” (2023):** lawyers submitted fabricated court decisions generated by ChatGPT; the court sanctioned the lawyers and their firm $5,000 (the June 22, 2023 order also required corrective letters). Expected decision: **continue only with strict safeguards** — AI may assist with drafting but must not act as the authority for citations; stop filing now and resume only in an independently verified workflow.
2. **Customer experience — “The Chatbot Promise” (2024):** an Air Canada chatbot incorrectly said a bereavement fare could be requested retroactively; a tribunal ordered compensation and rejected the idea that the airline was not responsible for its chatbot’s inaccurate information. Expected: **restricted** — pause the incorrect policy answers until verified content, monitoring and human escalation are in place.
3. **Health support — “When Advice Becomes Harm” (2023):** NEDA suspended Tessa after reports of potentially harmful weight-loss advice to people seeking eating-disorder support. Expected: **STOP** now — a rewritten prompt alone is not a safe restart; testing and expert governance may support future limited tools.
Across the cases the genuine root causes are the same kinds of gap — verification, oversight / authority, escalation — and the effective safeguards work independently of the model’s own claims.

## Debrief questions (suggested)
1. Which exhibit changed your mind the most?
2. Where in your own workplace could a similar failure reach a customer, a court or a vulnerable person — and who would catch it?
3. When is STOP the right call, and when is restricted use enough? What made case 3 different?
4. Which safeguard works *without* trusting the AI’s own claims? Which popular fixes (a better prompt, a disclaimer) do not?

## Notes
- Scoring follows the kit’s formulas (root causes, safeguards, decision) plus a transparent 10-point report part; it is coaching, not an expert verdict. Use it for feedback, not for grading people.
